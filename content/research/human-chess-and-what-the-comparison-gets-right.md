## 0. Hard constraints and how they were met

- **The phrase is attributed to nobody.** Four pages are quoted as their publishers' own wording. None names an originator and no page read gives a first use or date.
- **Chase and Simon (1973) and de Groot are not characterised.** The 1973 finding appears only in the words of Bartlett, Boggan and Krawczyk (2013), and the article says the 1973 paper was not read. De Groot is not mentioned at all: no source read states his findings in a form that could be restated.
- **No claim about intelligence, no ranking of people, no advice.** The fatigue sentence explicitly makes no claim about decisions.
- **No study compares chess players with grapplers** in the searching done; the last section says so and limits it to one search on one day.

## 3. Contradictions and how each was handled

- **Peric et al. contradict themselves on reaction time.** The abstract says the professional group showed "slower reaction times"; the Results text reports professionals "responding more rapidly (M = 232.2 ms) than semi-professionals (M = 293.0 ms)", not significant (p = 0.169). The article states no direction and prints neither number; `contestedNotes` records why.
- **Russo and Ottoboni's sport tally sums to 36 against 31 articles.** Presumably some articles cover more than one sport, but the manuscript does not say so in the passage read. The article prints the tally "as printed" and offers no explanation.
- **Experts versus whom.** The review's abstract compares experts with novices; its conclusion says "experts compared to non-athletes". Both are given in the source's own terms.
- **Laboratory tests versus performance.** The combat sports review reports expert advantages on tests; the soccer study found test scores did not predict play among similarly skilled players. Both are printed; the article draws only the narrow conclusion that the two are different measurements.
- **Bartlett et al. is not the classic study.** Its task is recognition (one-back), not recall. The article keeps the quoted recall finding and the paper's own accuracy figures in separate paragraphs and says the task "is not a game".

## 7. Claims cut, and why

- **Anything about de Groot.** No source read states his findings.
- **How many chunks an expert holds, or how many years expertise takes.** Figures of that kind circulate; none was in a source read.
- **Leone et al.'s study of internet games** (reported by Connors and Campitelli). Second hand, and not needed.
- **The fMRI findings of Bartlett et al.** The piece is not about brain regions, and the behavioural result carries the point.
- **The blindfolded judo study and the karate repeated-attack study** described by Krabben et al. Both second hand; cut for length.
- **Peric et al.'s direction of effect and reaction times.** See section 3.
- **"Complete information" as a term of art.** Replaced by the plain statement that both players see the whole board, labelled ours.
- **Any claim that grapplers rely on touch as a research finding.** Stated as ours; no haptic study was read.
- **Any claim that chess or jiu-jitsu improves thinking, or that either attracts or produces intelligent people.** Out of scope and unsourceable.
- **Any statement of how common the nickname is.** Four pages are described as four pages.

5. The Peric et al. internal contradiction on reaction time: confirm the article's decision to state no direction.
