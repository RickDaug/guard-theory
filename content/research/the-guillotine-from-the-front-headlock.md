## 3. Contradictory accounts — how each was handled

- **The can opener.** ADCC lists it under legal techniques ("Can Opener is allowed"). UWW names it as an example of a prohibited neck crank. A flat contradiction between two current rule books. The article prints both in the same paragraph and says the documents do not agree. `contestedNotes` repeats it. **Do not reconcile these; the disagreement is the point.**
- **Why UWW excepts the arm-in guillotine for juniors.** The document states the exception and gives no reason. The article reports the rule and explicitly says in `contestedNotes` that this is not evidence the arm-in version carries less risk. Resisting the obvious inference was the main editorial decision in this piece.
- **Arm-in vs arm-out finishing mechanics.** Coaching sources disagree about which finishes more readily. Not adjudicated; no frequency claim appears.

## 9. Fact-check status

**Passed, with the following cut:**

- *Cut:* every physiological account of what a guillotine does. Risk is described as a lever applied to a held head, plus what the rule books restrict, plus the slam hazard, which is mechanical.
- *Cut:* the inference that UWW excepts the arm-in guillotine *because* it is safer. The document does not say so and no other source does.
- *Cut:* the claim that the guillotine is one of the most common finishes in MMA or in grappling. There is a published analysis of fight-ending chokes in one MMA promotion, but it sits in a medical journal, it reports competition statistics, and both categories are excluded by this commission. Not cited.
- *Cut:* every division assignment from the IBJJF illegal-moves table. Column marks are images; only row captions extract.
- *Cut:* any origin story or naming attribution.
- *Cut:* a planned section on the guillotine as a takedown counter and from closed guard. Both set different problems; the article says so in §1 and stays with the front headlock, per the commission's instruction that each set-up deserves its own treatment.
- *Cut:* "most guillotines fail because people pull with the arms" — a frequency claim. Rewritten as a description of the failure and a diagnostic.

## 10. Editorial notes

- §2 leads with the rule rather than with the mechanics, because the rule is the stronger evidence and because leading with mechanics would have made the arm-in point look like a preference.
- §5 exists because this is one of the few submissions where the attacker is more often the one hurt, and no article about it is honest without saying so.
- The default given to beginners — learn arm-in first — is offered as a default in the absence of coaching, not as a claim about relative risk. Keep that framing if the paragraph is edited.
