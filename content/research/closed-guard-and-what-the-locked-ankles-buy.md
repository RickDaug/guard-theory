## 0. The brief's hard constraints

- "Commonly taught first" had to be sourced to published words or softened. It is sourced: Kesting and Bayev's glossary says the basic closed guard "is the style of guard most often taught to beginners". The article prints it as their statement, says it is not a survey, and records that in `contestedNotes`.
- Mechanics, not history. Nothing in the article says where the position came from. Kesting's "the oldest guard of all" and his remark about who introduced the position to North America were read and left out (section 6).
- No medical, injury-prevention or safety advice. Two sources contain some (Kesting on strikes from a relaxed closed guard, on flexibility for the rubber guard; Scully on where to pull on the head). None was carried over. The Library's own safety note on being lifted is not restated.
- No invented statistic, no ranking of people, no competition record.
- Consistency with the Library entries `closed-guard-posture-battle`, `armbar-is-hip-to-shoulder-distance`, `triangle-closes-at-the-shoulder`, `guillotine-is-a-broken-posture`, and with the Journal entries named in `relatedSlugs`. See section 3.

## 3. Contradictions and consistency — how each was handled

- **Posture has two meanings.** The Library uses posture for the alignment of the spine; Kesting also uses it for arm and body position inside the guard (recorded in the Journal's `base-posture-and-structure`). This article uses the Library's sense throughout and says so in `contestedNotes`.
- **Kneeling or standing to open.** Kesting's two passing articles prefer standing against skilled opponents and say kneeling breaks also work. The article takes no side; it only uses his remark about crossed ankles tiring the legs when the passer stands, and the Library's line that a standing opponent has won the posture exchange.
- **"Offering great control" against "only an attachment".** The glossary says the closed guard offers "great control"; the Library says it "looks like control but is only an attachment". These are not in conflict once posture is taken into account (the Library's point is that the control is conditional on posture), and the glossary phrase is not quoted in the final text.
- **Seated/supine piece.** That article's 29 September correction removed an IBJJF closed guard stalling example that had been applied to open guard. Here the same examples are applied to closed guard, which is what they are written for.
- **Armbar and triangle pieces.** Both cite IBJJF 6.2.2 W (jumping to closed guard). Not repeated here; this piece is about the position once held.
- **ADCC page has no version.** Cited as read on the accessed date, as the other Journal entries do.

## 6. Claims cut, and why

- **Closed guard as "the oldest guard of all"** (Kesting, *A Closed Guard Passing Strategy*) and **who introduced it to North America** (*When the Closed Guard Fails*). History, out of scope for this piece, and neither is a claim this site has sourced independently.
- **Anything about strikes, MMA or self-defence.** The glossary and the principles article both discuss keeping posture broken against strikes. That is a different subject with a safety edge to it, and the Journal has an MMA category for it.
- **"Lock inside the closed guard with legs compressing kidneys or ribs"** appears as a row in the IBJJF table of illegal moves. `pdftotext` does not preserve which division columns are shaded for that row and no PDF renderer was available, so the divisions it applies to could not be read. Cut entirely. The owner may want it added after looking at the table on page 29 of the PDF.
- **IBJJF 1.3.8 and the 4.1.12 note** (referee positioning in under-12 matches when a child is lifted in closed guard; a pulled closed guard suspended in the air). Both are about being lifted; left out to keep clear of safety territory.
- **Which named champions stand to open the guard.** In Kesting's guard-break article. Not needed, and close to ranking people.
- **"a beginner tends to neglect" the legs.** Written in a first draft as a gloss on Kesting's leg-pull point; it was an unsourced generalisation and was replaced with "singles out the legs".
- **A sentence that ADCC leaves a locked guard with a held head "to the referee".** True by implication but not printed anywhere; cut.
- **Any claim about how often closed guard attacks or passes succeed, in or out of the gi.** No data located; none claimed.
