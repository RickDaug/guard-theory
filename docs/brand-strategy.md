# Brand strategy

Who this is for, what it claims, and what it refuses to claim.

Grounded in `docs/competitor-research.md`. Where a claim here rests on
research, it says so; where it is a judgement, it says that too.

---

## The one idea

**Great grappling is not random.** Every grip, frame, angle and reaction is
part of a larger system. Guard is not a position — it is a theory of control.

Everything the brand makes and publishes is downstream of taking that
seriously, including the decision to publish specifications instead of
adjectives.

## Who it is for

**The grappler who reads.** Training two to five times a week, no-gi first or
no-gi only, somewhere between eighteen months and a decade in. They already
own kit that works. They watch instructionals, argue about leg entanglement
rules, and can tell you why a pass failed.

They are not looking for motivation. They are looking for an explanation.

**What they are underserved on.** The research found no apparel brand in the
category publishing a structured technique reference. Shoyoroll's technique
series is video embeds with near-empty article bodies. Scramble's technique
vertical stopped in February 2019. Of thirteen brands studied, five publish
nothing, and four more built a channel and let it rot. The genuine written
references in jiu-jitsu sit entirely outside apparel.

That is the gap: not another shop, and not another blog — a **reference that
happens to sell things**.

**Who it is not for.** Someone buying their first rash guard on price. Someone
who wants a brand as an identity badge. Both are legitimate customers for
someone else.

## Position

> Guard Theory makes no-gi grappling apparel and publishes the reasoning
> behind it. Position before submission. Systems before chaos.

**What that commits us to.** If the writing is not genuinely worth citing, the
position collapses — the apparel is then just apparel with a blog, which is
what the category already has too much of.

## What the category has taught us

Three findings shaped the strategy:

**Restraint is the rulebook, not a position.** IBJJF requires no-gi rash
guards to be black or white with at least 10% belt-rank colour. A brand cannot
differentiate on colour because the category cannot use colour. Anyone selling
"minimalism" as their identity is describing a constraint they had no say in.
Differentiation has to come from system and architecture.

**Editorial decay is the category's defining failure mode.** That is what makes
the Journal defensible and what makes it a real commitment. A Journal that
stops in eighteen months does more damage than never starting one.

**The register is contested.** Hyperfly's "Grapplism" already frames grappling
as an art movement and invokes brutalism — then ships t-shirts and a blog post.
The position is only winnable by having the substance they do not.

## Personality

**Is:** intelligent · technical · modern · confident · restrained · cultured ·
competitive · slightly mysterious · design-conscious.

**Is not:** corny · loud without purpose · full of warrior clichés · decorated
with flames, skulls or invented Japanese symbolism · so aggressively masculine
it excludes customers · a streetwear brand · an MMA brand · gym merchandise.

## Voice

Plain verbs. Sentence case. Vary sentence length. Say the number instead of the
adjective. Where something is unknown, say it is unknown in the same tone as
everything else — no apology, no euphemism.

The brand never tells you what to feel about jiu-jitsu. It tells you how
something works and lets that be the appeal.

## What the brand refuses

These are positions, not omissions, and each is enforced somewhere in the
codebase:

- **No invented facts.** No price, date, measurement, statistic, byline or
  founder story exists until it is real.
- **No manufactured urgency.** No countdown, no stock counter, no discount
  wheel, no fake activity.
- **No borrowed imagery.** Nothing scraped, no athlete photography without
  rights, no stock gym, no generated garment passed off as real.
- **No commercial links in reference material.** Nobody forwards an advert.
- **No affiliate income, no sponsored posts.** If that changes, the disclosure
  page changes first.
- **No "greatest ever" claims.** They need criteria nobody agrees on.
- **No medical, hygiene or injury-prevention claims.** Ever.

## The commercial model

**Now.** One conversion: the First Edition waitlist. No payment, no
pre-order, no account.

**First.** Rash guards, long and short sleeve, in a single run that will be
described as finished rather than quietly restocked.

**Then.** Spats and shorts, once fit is proven on real bodies rather than on a
size chart.

**Later.** Accessories. Research says tape is validated across six brands and
is the item most easily specified honestly, so it comes first. Only two of
thirteen brands sell a mouthguard. Mats are a trap — one co-branded precedent
in the entire category.

**One unclaimed opening worth noting.** Shoyoroll's co-branded pen-and-notepad
set sold out; it is the only notational product in the category. A brand whose
signature is a notation system has an unusually credible claim on that space.
Recorded as an opportunity, not a plan.

## How we will know it is working

Not revenue, at this stage. The leading indicators are:

- Does anyone send a Technique Library entry to a training partner?
- Does anything get cited or linked by someone outside the brand?
- Does the waitlist grow from the writing rather than from paid reach?
- Do corrections arrive? A reference nobody corrects is a reference nobody
  reads carefully.

## The risk

The whole position rests on sustained editorial quality by a small team, in a
category where four brands with more resources started a channel and abandoned
it. Six finished articles is a beginning, not proof.

The mitigation is that the technique reference is finite and durable — twelve
areas of the game do not go stale — whereas a news-shaped blog needs feeding
forever. Build the reference first; let the Journal be the slower thing.
